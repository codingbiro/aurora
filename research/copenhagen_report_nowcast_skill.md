## 1. NOAA propagated solar wind and Geospace (SWMF) model

### 1.1 What the propagated product is (VERIFIED)
- SWPC product page "Geospace Geomagnetic Activity Plot" (https://www.spaceweather.gov/products/geospace-geomagnetic-activity-plot, fetched 2026-09-24; swpc.noaa.gov URL 301-redirects there): "Real-time solar wind values ballistically propagated from L1 orbit to 32 Re at the upstream boundary" drive the Michigan Geospace model; the model "can provide Kp and Dst forecasts with approximately 30 to 60 minutes advanced warning, depending on the speed of the solar wind"; v2.0 (operational since Feb 2021) uses "a new method for calculating a predicted estimated Kp (global geomagnetic activity index) based on magnetic variations from the Geospace model processed by a Kp algorithm"; components BATS-R-US + RIM + RCM; "Cold restarts of the Geospace model can occur when there is no solar wind data for more than 2 hours". The page states the plots "do not display the official SWPC forecast".
- Directory https://services.swpc.noaa.gov/products/geospace/ (fetched 2026-09-24 17:32 UTC stamp) contains only `propagated-solar-wind.json` (1.1 MB) and `propagated-solar-wind-1-hour.json` (6.2 KB). There is no product-description document for the JSON itself; the only public description of the method is the one-liner above ("ballistically propagated ... to 32 Re").
- Propagation method (VERIFIED, Cash et al. 2016 abstract via OpenAlex, DOI 10.1002/2015SW001321, https://api.openalex.org/works?search=Validation%20of%20an%20operational%20product%20to%20determine%20L1%20to%20Earth%20propagation%20time%20delays): "A simple flat plane convection delay method is currently used by NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center (SWPC) to propagate the solar wind from a monitoring satellite located at L1 [to a] point upstream of the magnetosphere. This technique assumes that all observed solar wind discontinuities ... are in a plane perpendicular to the Sun-Earth line traveling in [the] GSE X direction at [the observed] velocity. In reality, these phase fronts can have significantly tilted orientations, and relying on a ballistic propagation delay method, time errors [of] +/-15 min are common." Their tilted-phase-front (Weimer & King 2008) tool showed "no significant difference between the two propagation methods when run in [a] real-time operational environment." (NOAA repository landing page https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/15197 confirms title/authors/year; the PDF link served an unrelated document, so the body statistics are UNVERIFIED.)
- Note for the dashboard: the flat-plane delay is distance_X / v_x. The dashboard's "arrival stamps 30-88 min ahead, median 56 min" is consistent with 1.5e6 km / (300-800 km/s) minus the 32 Re offset, i.e. the propagated product is NOT a forecast but a time-shift, and the +/-15 min timing error of Cash et al. is the irreducible timing noise on every "measured" minute of driving.

## 3. Kp nowcast/forecast from L1 solar wind: published skill vs lead time and vs persistence

### 3.1 Wintoft et al. 2017 (VERIFIED, full text from https://gfzpublic.gfz.de/rest/items/item_2824943_5/component/file_2837900/content; JSWSC 7, A29, DOI 10.1051/swsc/2017027)
- Lead time: "The models (ensemble of networks) provide prediction lead times in the range 20-90 min given by the time it takes a solar wind structure to travel from L1 to Earth." Propagation: t = r/v with r the distance from ACE to 10 RE; "The observed propagation lead times t are distributed bi-modally with peaks at 44 and 58 min, and with minimum and maximum at 20 and 90 min, respectively."
- Skill: "The IRF-Kp-2017 model has RMS error of 0.55 and linear correlation of 0.92 based on an independent test set with final Kp covering 2 years using ACE Level 2 data. The IRF-Kp-2017-h3 model has RMSE = 0.63 and correlation = 0.89. ... tested on another two-year period with real-time ACE data which gives RMSE = 0.59 for IRF-Kp-2017 and RMSE = 0.73 for IRF-Kp-2017-h3."
- On persistence: "We do not consider persistence a useful model in itself, however, on many scores it performs very well due to autocorrelations in the series. ... the Boberg et al. (2000) (IRF-Kp-2000) performs poorer than the persistence model with respect to BIAS and MAE, while the RMSE are similar".
- On the lead-time ceiling (SSC example, Kp went 3 -> 6+ within the 10 min 22:50-23:00 UT): "we cannot expect, in the general case, that any further prediction lead time is possible than that given by the propagation lead time, when measured solar wind data is used."

### 3.2 Shprits et al. 2019, Space Weather 17, 1219, DOI 10.1029/2018SW002141 (abstract VERIFIED via OpenAlex)
- "for short-term forecasts of approximately half a day, the addition of measured solar wind values provides barely noticeable improvement" over history-based (recurrence/persistence) NN; "paucity of high Kp [results] in a lower accuracy of predictions during disturbed conditions".

## 5. Substorm timing as the limiting factor at mid-latitude

### 5.1 Onset predictability (VERIFIED)
- Maimaiti et al. 2019, Space Weather, DOI 10.1029/2019SW002251 (full text fetched from https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10189693; abstract also via OpenAlex): "the first deep learning-based time series model to forecast the onset of a magnetic substorm over the next 1 hr"; "developed using a comprehensive list of onsets compiled between 1997 and 2017 and achieves 72% precision and 77% recall"; inputs are a time history of solar wind Vx, density and IMF; "can correctly identify substorms ~75% of the time. In contrast, an earlier prediction algorithm correctly identifies ~21% of the same data set"; "solar wind alone may not be sufficient to forecast all substorms, and preconditioning of the magnetotail may be an important factor."
- Nakano et al. 2023, Ann. Geophys. 41, 529, DOI 10.5194/angeo-41-529-2023 (full text fetched from https://angeo.copernicus.org/articles/41/529/2023/angeo-41-529-2023.pdf): echo-state-network non-stationary Poisson model of substorm occurrence per hour from solar wind; "For the data from 2015 to 2018, the Brier score of the prediction with the ESN was 0.100. ... If a stationary Poisson process is assumed ... the probability of substorm occurrence per hour is 15.6%. Using this value, the Brier score with the stationary Poisson process becomes 0.136". (Reliability: "the predicted probability obtained with the ESN agrees well with the actual occurrence ratio except that the prediction slightly overestimates the occurrence ratio for 0.4 <= Pk < 0.8".) Note the modest gain: BSS vs climatology = 1 - 0.100/0.136 = 0.26.
- Interpretation for a 10-60 min site forecast: both published onset models resolve the hour, not the minute; they are probability-of-onset-within-60-min tools. Timing within the hour is unpredicted in the literature I could verify.

### 5.2 Where onset happens and how the boundary moves (VERIFIED)
- Frey et al. 2004, JGR, DOI 10.1029/2004JA010607 (OpenAlex): >2400 IMAGE-FUV onsets, "median substorm onset location at 2300 hours MLT and 66.4 degrees magnetic latitude".
- Milan et al. 2009, Ann. Geophys. 27, 659, DOI 10.5194/angeo-27-659-2009 (full text fetched from https://angeo.copernicus.org/articles/27/659/2009/angeo-27-659-2009.pdf): ~2000 IMAGE-FUV substorms binned by onset latitude (I >68 deg, II 66-68, III 64-66, IV 62-64, V <62 deg MLAT); "Most onsets were observed between 21:00 and 01:00 MLT"; "the poleward progression increases for decreasing onset latitude. The other meridians can display continued equatorward motion for between 20 min and 1 h after onset"; "the auroral oval can continue to progress equatorwards after onset at other local times (especially in the dawn sector)"; "IMF BZ becomes more negative prior to low latitude substorms, while VSW, NSW, and PSW all increase. The AL signature becomes more pronounced for the low latitude onsets."; dusk-sector proton oval "2 to 4 degrees of latitude equatorward of the dawn sector oval".
- Consequence for Copenhagen (52.4 deg MLAT, edge needed <= 60.4 deg): a category-V onset (<62 deg) is required before the expansion bulge can plausibly bring diffuse/red aurora within the 8-deg window; the expansion itself is mostly POLEWARD, while the equatorward edge keeps creeping equatorward for 20-60 min after onset (Milan 2009). So substorm onset changes brightness/altitude (visibility) more than it moves the equatorward edge, which is why the dashboard's "substorm-phase factor" multiplies P(edge within 8 deg) rather than shifting the edge. How many degrees the equatorward edge shifts at 55-60 deg during an expansion is NOT quantified in any source I could verify (Milan gives durations, not degrees): UNVERIFIED.

### 5.3 Finnish sector -> Denmark: can the IMAGE chain be a 10-20 min warning? (derived from VERIFIED speeds)
- Craven, Frank & Akasofu 1989, JGR 94, 6961, DOI 10.1029/JA094iA06p06961 (abstract via https://www.osti.gov/biblio/6048596): DE-1 imaging of a westward travelling surge over 7000 km: "average speed of the surge is 2.2 km/s"; "initially at a speed of about 8 km/s followed by a steady decline to about 1 km/s over a period of 17 min"; persistent bright regions "separated by approximately 700 km"; "the average time per advance is about 5 min".
- Geometry: 1 h of MLT = 15 deg magnetic longitude = 15 x 111 km x cos(MLAT) = 830 km at 60 deg MLAT (700 km at 65 deg). At 1-2.2 km/s a surge that starts over Finnish Lapland (100-107 deg mag. lon.) reaches the Danish meridian (~1 h MLT west) in 6-14 min; with the fast initial 8 km/s phase, as little as 2-3 min. So the IMAGE chain can give roughly 5-15 minutes of warning when (a) onset occurs east of Denmark and (b) the surge propagates westward - the common case pre-midnight, since onsets cluster 21-01 MLT (Milan 2009; Frey 2004 median 23 MLT) and Copenhagen is 1 h MLT earlier than Finland. When onset occurs at or west of Denmark's meridian (e.g. UK/Norwegian-sea sector) the chain gives no lead, only confirmation.
- Kauristie et al. 1996 (Ann. Geophys.) on what a single meridional chain can say about global electrojet activity: see citation block (abstract fetched via OpenAlex).
- What the chain cannot do: it sees the electrojet (currents at 60-70 deg MLAT), not the equatorward edge of diffuse aurora at 55-60 deg. The IL/IE index reaching e.g. -500 nT says a substorm is under way at 65-70 deg; whether aurora is visible from 55.7 deg N depends on the edge latitude and brightness, which the chain constrains only indirectly (Gussenhoven 1983: the edge is ordered by Kp/convection, not by AL).

## 6. Verification methodology and truth data for Denmark / N Germany

### 6.1 Methods used in the literature (VERIFIED)
- Machol 2012: contingency table of visible-aurora yes/no within the hour vs Polar UVI (77 % overall, 86 % for "will occur" forecasts).
- Mooney 2021: ROC (0.82 overall; 0.59 dayside; 0.55 at Kp 8), reliability (underprediction factor 1.1-6; >90 % overpredicted), truth = IMAGE FUV boundaries 2000-2002; forecast = 30-min probability of aurora.
- Case 2016 / Kosar 2018 / Gannon-storm survey 2024 (Geosci. ## Where the dashboard's chain loses skill and what would fix it (ranked by expected gain for Copenhagen at 10-60 min lead)

1. The Kp/Hp30 -> Starkov/OVATION-edge -> "edge within 8 deg" step is the weakest and least validated link. Nothing published maps Hp30 (or a 4-h weighted coupling) to an equatorward boundary; the Kp-driven statistical ovals carry ~1 deg per Kp step with "deteriorating" fits above Kp 6 (Carbary 2005), OP-13 and the Kp-driven Z-P boundary differ by ~1.5 deg on average (Kosar 2018), and the OP-2013 oval is only shown down to ~55 deg MLAT (Newell 2014), 2.6 deg from the observer. Each Hp30 third is ~0.3 deg of edge, so the RMSE 0.75-0.85 of the Hp30 regression (yours 0.75; Kervalishvili 0.85) is only ~0.25 deg of edge - the regression is not the bottleneck, the oval model is (1-2 deg of systematic disagreement between models, larger at Kp >= 6). What would fix it: (a) replace the 1 erg cm^-2 s^-1 electron edge with the low-energy/red-visibility edge (Case 2016 flux-based view line gave 91 % vs the brightest-aurora line; Gannon-storm reports were red/pink <1 keV and far equatorward of the models), (b) calibrate the edge offset locally against a proxy truth (AuroraWatch UK amber/red hours, polarlicht-archiv.de nights) rather than trusting Starkov's absolute latitude, (c) report the edge as a distribution (spread of Starkov vs OP-13 vs Z-P ~1.5-2 deg) and integrate over it instead of a 60/40 point blend.

2. Substorm timing is the dominant unmodelled variance at 10-60 min and is only predictable at the one-hour, probability level (Maimaiti: 72 %/77 % for onset in the next hour; Nakano: BSS 0.26 per hour). The IMAGE chain gives a real but short lead: with surge speeds 1-2.2 km/s and 1 h of MLT (~830 km) between Lapland and Denmark, a pre-midnight onset east of Denmark reaches the Danish meridian in ~6-14 min (2-3 min in the fast initial phase); onsets at or west of Denmark's meridian give no lead. What would fix it: (a) keep the chain-onset detector but treat it as a 5-15 min "now" signal that raises brightness/visibility, not the edge (Milan 2009: post-onset motion is poleward on the onset meridian, equatorward creep of 20-60 min elsewhere), (b) add an explicit P(onset in next 60 min) from the propagated solar wind (Maimaiti-style inputs: Vx, n, IMF history) and use it as the substorm-phase factor for lead times beyond the chain's reach, (c) do not let the chain electrojet index (which correlates with global AL only in ~1730-2230 and 0000-0400 MLT, Kauristie 1996) stand in for the equatorward edge at 55-60 deg.

3. The "measured driving" is not as measured as it looks: the flat-plane L1 shift carries +/-15 min timing errors (Cash 2016; SWPC still uses the flat-plane method, and the tilted-front alternative gave "no significant difference"), the propagated product's stamps are 52-61 min ahead at today's ~385 km/s but only ~20-30 min at 700-900 km/s (Wintoft 2017: 20-90 min), and the magnetosphere adds its own ~30 min lag (Luo 2017). What would fix it: (a) treat the per-minute coupling as a time-uncertain series (smear each minute by +/-15 min before the 4-h weighting; the weighting itself already hides most of the timing error, which is why Wintoft/Luo get RMSE 0.5-0.6 with 3-h averages), (b) shrink the "measured" horizon by 15 min for shocks/sector boundaries, (c) blend the Geospace Kp with a weight that decays to zero at its last stamp (+35 min today) rather than extrapolating it.

4. Persistence anchoring is right but the published numbers say the anchor should dominate for longer than a decaying weight suggests: with a 3-h index, persistence RMSE is 0.89 vs 0.52-0.59 for the best solar-wind models (Wintoft 2017), but at 30-min resolution your own persistence RMSE (0.62) already beats the coupling regression (0.75), and Shprits 2019 found that adding measured solar wind to history-based NN gives "barely noticeable improvement" for short-term forecasts. What would fix it: fit the blend weight w(lead) on your own archive by minimising RMSE of w*persistence + (1-w)*regression per lead bin (0-30, 30-60 min); expect w > 0.5 through 30 min and the coupling term to matter mainly when the 4-h weighted coupling is changing fast (SSC/CME arrival, Wintoft's Kp 3 -> 6+ in 10 min example).

5. The GFZ Hpo-forecast member is currently an empty promise for the short range: the `aceprop` (L1-driven) Hp30 file held only -1 values, the `swpc`/`enlil` members are day-scale ambient-wind drivers refreshed hourly, and their first step (1.33) sat 0.7-2.0 thirds below the observed Hp30 at the same time. No method or verification is published for v0102 (no README anywhere in the ISDC tree). What would fix it: drop the ISDC members from the 0-60 min blend (keep them for the 2-72 h outlook), or gate them on `aceprop` being non-missing and on agreement with the last observed Hp30 within one third.

6. Verification is the missing feedback loop and is cheap to add: log the 10/30/60-min P(visible) and the inputs; use as proxy truth (i) AuroraWatch UK hourly level (thresholds 50/100/200 nT; archive since 2000 per Case 2017, API live) mapped to Copenhagen's MLAT, (ii) polarlicht-archiv.de event nights and Skywarden reports for the Finnish sector, (iii) your own Hp30 and OVATION-edge exceedances as a self-consistency check; compute Brier/BSS vs a climatology per MLT-season bin, ROC and a reliability diagram exactly as Mooney 2021 and Nakano 2023 do. Expect ROC ~0.8 to be the ceiling with Kp-class inputs (OP-2013 ROC 0.82; Aurora Hunter stage-1 0.849 at Tromso where aurora is common) and calibrate rather than chase it. No Danish all-sky camera or public Danish sightings archive with times could be verified; the German AKM archive is the nearest, and its table is client-rendered (no API found), so a scrape or a data request is needed before it can serve as truth.

Lower priority: the 3-model Kp blend (Geospace, GFZ ISDC, your regression) mixes products with different lead definitions; Geospace Kp in quiet times was overpredicted by 1-1.7 Kp in the v1-era study (Haiducek 2017) and the v2.0 evaluation statistics could not be fetched, so give it an empirically fitted bias correction and a small weight until you have measured it against observed Hp30 yourself.
